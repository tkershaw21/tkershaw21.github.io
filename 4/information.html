Understanding our neighborhood

Solar System Essentials

Learn the ideas that connect the different worlds in Solar System Explorer.

How the solar system formed

The solar system formed about 4.6 billion years ago from a cloud of gas and dust. Gravity pulled material together, and most of it collected in the center to form the Sun.

Remaining material spread into a disk. Collisions and gravitational attraction helped smaller pieces become larger objects. The warm inner region favored rocky materials, while colder outer regions allowed more ice to survive.

Planets, moons, asteroids, and comets preserve different parts of this history. Small objects can contain clues that larger, more active worlds have erased.

Source: NASA Solar System Facts

Gravity and orbits

Gravity attracts objects toward one another. An orbit combines that inward pull with forward motion. A moving planet continuously falls around the Sun instead of traveling straight away.

Orbits are ellipses, although many are close to circles. An object's distance therefore changes as it moves. This guide uses rounded average distances instead of live positions.

Rotation and orbiting are different. Rotation means turning on an axis. Orbiting means traveling around another object.

Different kinds of worlds

Planets orbit the Sun, are nearly round, and dominate their orbital neighborhoods.

Dwarf planets orbit the Sun and are rounded by gravity, but have not cleared their neighborhoods. They are not moons.

Moons are natural satellites. Their classification depends on what they orbit, not simply their size.

Small bodies include most asteroids and comets. Asteroids are generally rocky or metallic, while comets contain ice that can release gas and dust.

Comparing the rocky planets

An astronomical unit, or AU, is approximately the average distance between Earth and the Sun: about 150 million kilometers.

Rounded reference values for the inner planets
Planet Diameter (km) Average Sun distance (AU) Year length
Mercury 4,880 0.39 88 Earth days
Venus 12,104 0.72 225 Earth days
Earth 12,756 1.00 365.25 days
Mars 6,780 1.52 687 Earth days

Earth and Venus values are equatorial diameters. Sources are linked on the individual planet pages.

How spacecraft help us learn

Different missions answer different questions. A flyby records details during a brief encounter. An orbiter can monitor a world over time. A lander or rover can investigate material directly at the surface.

  • NEAR Shoemaker: orbited Eros in 2000 and landed in 2001.
  • Dawn: studied Vesta from 2011 to 2012 before traveling to Ceres.
  • New Horizons: flew past Pluto in 2015 and Arrokoth in 2019.

Not every space image is a natural-color photograph. Some images enhance differences in surface materials. Others are composites or artists' concepts. Read the captions to understand what an image represents.
