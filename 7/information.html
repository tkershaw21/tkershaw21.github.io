Our beginnings

The solar system formed about 4.6 billion years ago from a cloud of gas and dust. As gravity drew material together, most collected in the center to form the Sun. Remaining material spread into a disk, where collisions and gravitational attraction helped build larger objects.

The warmer inner region favored rocky materials. Farther out, colder conditions allowed more ice to survive. The planets, moons, and smaller objects we study today preserve different parts of this history. An asteroid or comet can therefore offer evidence that a large, geologically active planet has erased.

Gravity & orbits

Gravity pulls objects toward one another. An orbit combines that inward pull with forward motion: a moving world keeps falling around the object it orbits. Planets travel around the Sun, while moons orbit planets or other bodies.

Orbits are ellipses, although many are close to circles. Distance therefore changes throughout an orbit. The guide uses rounded averages or general locations to avoid suggesting that an object stays at one fixed distance. Rotation is different from orbiting: rotation is a world turning on its own axis.

Types of objects

Planets orbit the Sun, are nearly round, and dominate their orbital neighborhoods gravitationally. The eight planets include four rocky terrestrial planets and four giant planets.

Dwarf planets also orbit the Sun and are rounded by gravity, but have not cleared their neighborhoods. They are not moons. Pluto and Ceres belong to this group even though their locations and histories differ greatly.

Moons are natural satellites. A moon can be larger than a planet—Ganymede is wider than Mercury—because the classification depends on what it orbits, not just its size.

Small solar system bodies include most asteroids and comets. Asteroids are generally rocky or metallic; comets contain substantial ice that can release gas and dust when warmed. Small Kuiper Belt objects such as Arrokoth preserve materials from the distant outer solar system.

Reading the numbers

An astronomical unit (AU) is approximately the average Earth–Sun distance, about 150 million kilometers. A planet 2 AU from the Sun is about twice as far out as Earth. For a moon, its distance from its parent planet is often more useful than its distance from the Sun.

A diameter measures the width through the center of an object. Some worlds are not perfectly round, so a source may report an equatorial or mean diameter. For irregular bodies, several dimensions communicate the shape more clearly.

How we explore

Different missions answer different questions. A telescope can monitor a distant world repeatedly. A flyby records details during a brief encounter. An orbiter observes a world over time, and a lander or rover can examine material directly at the surface.

Images also require careful interpretation. Some show approximately natural colors, others enhance differences that would be difficult to see, and some distant objects are represented by artists’ concepts. Check each caption before treating an illustration as a photograph.
