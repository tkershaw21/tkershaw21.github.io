Understanding our neighborhood

Solar System Essentials

Learn how the solar system formed, what keeps objects in orbit, and how scientists investigate distant worlds. Use these ideas to understand the objects you discover on the Explore page.

How the solar system formed

The solar system formed about 4.6 billion years ago from a cloud of gas and dust. Gravity pulled material together, and most of it collected in the center to form the Sun.

Remaining material spread into a disk. Collisions and gravitational attraction helped smaller pieces become larger objects. The warm inner region favored rocky materials, while colder outer regions allowed more ice to survive.

Planets, moons, asteroids, and comets preserve different parts of this history. Small objects can contain clues that larger, more active worlds have erased.

Source: NASA Solar System Facts

Gravity and orbits

Gravity attracts objects toward one another. An orbit combines that inward pull with forward motion. A moving planet continuously falls around the Sun instead of traveling straight away.

Planetary orbits are ellipses, although many are close to circles. An object's distance from the Sun therefore changes as it moves. Average distances describe an orbit without giving the object's position at a particular moment.

Rotation and orbiting are different. Rotation means turning on an axis. Orbiting means traveling around another object.

Different kinds of worlds

Planets orbit the Sun, are nearly round, and dominate their orbital neighborhoods.

Dwarf planets orbit the Sun and are rounded by gravity, but have not cleared their orbital neighborhoods. They are not moons.

Moons are natural satellites. Their classification depends on what they orbit, not simply their size.

Small bodies include most asteroids and comets. Asteroids are generally rocky or metallic, while comets contain ice that can release gas and dust.

Understanding space measurements

Scientists use different measurements to describe the size, location, and motion of objects in space. Checking the measurement's label helps you make meaningful comparisons.

  • Diameter: the distance across an object through its center.
  • Radius: the distance from the center to the surface. For a sphere, the diameter is twice the radius.
  • Astronomical unit: a distance approximately equal to the average distance between Earth and the Sun.
  • Orbital period: the time needed to complete one orbit around another object.

Images on this website are not displayed to a common scale. Two objects shown in equally sized pictures may have very different actual sizes.

How spacecraft help us learn

Different missions answer different questions. Scientists choose spacecraft and instruments based on what they want to investigate.

  • Flyby: a spacecraft passes an object and collects information during the encounter.
  • Orbiter: a spacecraft travels around an object, allowing repeated observations over time.
  • Lander: a spacecraft reaches the surface and studies conditions at its landing location.
  • Rover: a vehicle moves across the surface to examine different locations.

Cameras reveal surface features, while other instruments investigate temperature, composition, atmospheres, or magnetic fields. Combining these measurements helps scientists build a more complete understanding of a world.

Reading space images

Not every space image is a natural-color photograph. Some images enhance differences in surface materials. Others combine several observations into one view or show an artist's interpretation.

Read the captions and linked image sources to understand what an image represents. An illustration can explain an idea, but it should not be mistaken for a photograph taken by a spacecraft.
